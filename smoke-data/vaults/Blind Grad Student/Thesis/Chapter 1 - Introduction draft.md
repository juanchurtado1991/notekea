## 1.1 Kelp forests and why they matter
Kelp forests line roughly a quarter of the world's coastlines and are among the most productive habitats on Earth.
They provide food and shelter for fish, invertebrates and marine mammals, protect shorelines from wave energy and
support fisheries worth a large share of coastal economies. Along temperate coasts of the northeast Pacific, the giant
and bull kelps form a canopy that changes light, flow and temperature for everything living beneath it.

## 1.2 From forest to barren
The same coasts show a striking alternative state: the urchin barren. When sea urchins graze down a forest, they can
hold the reef in a bare state for decades. The switch between the two states depends on urchin behaviour, predators
and the supply of drift kelp ([[Arden 2023 - Kelp recovery after marine heatwaves]]). Barrens now cover large
stretches of the northern coast ([[Mbeki 2025 - Urchin barrens mapping]]).

## 1.3 Marine heatwaves
Marine heatwaves are periods of unusually warm water lasting from days to months. They have become longer and more
frequent over the last four decades and are expected to keep doing so. The 2014–2016 event in the northeast Pacific
raised surface temperatures by up to 3 °C above normal for many months and coincided with large losses of kelp.

## 1.4 Larvae as the bottleneck
Most work on urchins and heat focuses on adults, which tolerate a wide range of temperatures. Larvae spend weeks in
the plankton and may be far more sensitive. If heatwaves kill larvae, they might limit the number of new urchins that
settle on reefs after the event, which in turn could give kelp a window to recover. This is the central idea of my
thesis and the reason Chapter 2 measures larval limits directly ([[Kowalski 2024 - Larval heat tolerance]]).

## 1.5 Aims
1. Measure the upper thermal limit of purple sea urchin larvae at several stages (Chapter 2).
2. Measure how warming changes adult grazing on kelp (Chapter 3).
3. Combine larval survival and grazing into a model of kelp recovery after heatwaves (Chapter 4).
