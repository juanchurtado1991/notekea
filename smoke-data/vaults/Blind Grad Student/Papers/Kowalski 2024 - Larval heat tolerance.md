# Kowalski 2024 – Larval heat tolerance in purple sea urchins

## Why I read it
This is the closest paper to my Chapter 2. Helen called it "the paper you have to beat" at our first meeting. It
looks at how the upper thermal limit of purple sea urchin larvae changes as they develop, which is exactly the
question behind [[Experiment 01 - Thermal ramp]].

## Summary
The authors reared larvae from wild-collected adults at a common temperature (15 °C) and then exposed batches at four
developmental stages (blastula, gastrula, prism and four-arm pluteus) to acute temperature ramps. Survival was scored
after 48 hours at the target temperature. They fitted logistic curves to get an LT50 (the temperature at which half
the larvae die) for each stage, and they compared parents from two populations: a northern cold site and a southern
warm site.

## Methods (the parts I care about)
- Parents: 12 females and 10 males per population, spawned with KCl injection.
- Rearing density: 5 larvae per mL, fed a mixed algal diet from the prism stage on.
- Ramp: temperature raised by 1 °C per hour from 15 °C to the target, then held for 48 hours.
- Targets: 15, 18, 21, 23, 25, 27 and 29 °C, with five replicate beakers per target.
- Survival scored under a dissecting scope on a 1 mL subsample, counted twice by two people.
- Statistics: logistic regression per stage, with population as a fixed effect and family as a random effect.

They make a big point that the 1 °C per hour ramp is slow enough for larvae to start a heat shock response, so their
limits are probably a bit higher than a sudden shock would give. Worth a sentence in my methods.

## Results
The early stages were the most fragile. Blastulae and gastrulae died at temperatures that the later stages handled
easily, which the authors put down to the cost of building the skeleton and the lack of a gut. They show this in
Figure 2 as a staircase of curves moving right as the larvae develop.

Population mattered less than I expected. The southern larvae had LT50s on average only 0.6 °C higher than the
northern ones, and the difference was not significant for the two later stages. They argue that local adaptation in
thermal tolerance is weak because larvae drift far between populations.

Figure 3 is the one I keep going back to. For the later stages the authors give the fitted limits in the text, not
just in the plot: the LT50 for the four-arm pluteus stage was 25.8 °C, compared with 27.1 °C for the prism stage. So
the pluteus is more sensitive than the prism, which goes against the simple "older is tougher" story from the early
stages. They think the arms (long, thin, with a lot of surface) are the weak point.

There is also a short section on sublethal effects. At 23 °C, survival was almost normal but plutei had arms about
15 % shorter after 48 hours, and those larvae swam more slowly in a flume test.

## Discussion points
- Ocean temperatures during the 2014–2016 heatwave reached 23–24 °C at the surface in the southern part of the range,
  which is close to the sublethal threshold but under the LT50s.
- They warn that a 48 hour exposure is shorter than a real heatwave, which can last weeks.
- They suggest repeating the work with longer exposures and with food limitation.
