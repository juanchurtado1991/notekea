# Sleep and memory, reading notes

Notes for the seminar of 18 November. Eight studies, in the order of the reading list. For each: what they did, what they found, what I take from it, and my doubts.

## 1. Okafor Lab, word pairs

**What they did and found.** 48 students learned 40 word pairs at 21:00. Half slept 8 hours, half stayed awake until morning. The sleepers recalled 31 pairs on average, the others 22.

**What I take from it.** Sleep right after learning protects what was learned.

My doubt: the test was the morning after, so I cannot tell a protected memory from a rested head. I would like a second test a week later, with both groups rested.

**Details.** Design: two groups of 24, assigned by lot; tested at 9:00 the next day and again after 48 hours, when the gap was 29 against 23 pairs. The awake group played board games in a lit room with a researcher present. Nobody was allowed coffee. The pairs were unrelated nouns in the students' own language.

**For the seminar.** How would this study change what I do the night before an exam, and the afternoon before? I should bring one concrete habit from it, and one reason to distrust it. Method to remember: who was tested, how long after learning, and against what.

## 2. Lindqvist group, afternoon naps

**What they did and found.** 36 adults learned a route through a virtual town and then either took a 90-minute nap or watched a documentary. The nap group found the route 28 % faster in the evening.

**What I take from it.** A nap with deep sleep helps spatial memory the same day.

My doubt: the group that did not sleep also did something else for that time, and what they did may have interfered with the memory on its own.

**Details.** Design: 18 in each group; the nap was in a dark room with sleep recorded, and 14 of the 18 reached deep sleep. Those four who did not were no faster than the documentary group. The route had 12 turns, and the test was at 19:00, four hours after waking.

**For the seminar.** How would this study change what I do the night before an exam, and the afternoon before? I should bring one concrete habit from it, and one reason to distrust it. Method to remember: who was tested, how long after learning, and against what.

## 3. Marrero and Pinto, shift nurses

**What they did and found.** A survey and a memory test of 212 night-shift nurses over 6 weeks. After three nights in a row, scores on a list-learning test fell by 17 %, and came back after two nights of normal sleep.

**What I take from it.** Lost sleep adds up over days, and the loss is recovered.

My doubt: it is a survey of people who chose their work, so those who cope badly with nights may have left the ward long ago.

**Details.** Design: nurses of three hospitals, tested at the end of each run of shifts on a 15-word list read five times. Those over 45 lost more (21 %) than those under 30 (12 %). Naps during the shift were not recorded, which the authors count as their main gap.

**For the seminar.** How would this study change what I do the night before an exam, and the afternoon before? I should bring one concrete habit from it, and one reason to distrust it. Method to remember: who was tested, how long after learning, and against what.

## 4. Tanaka sleep unit, sounds in deep sleep

**What they did and found.** 22 volunteers heard soft clicks timed to their slow brain waves during deep sleep. Recall of word pairs the next morning rose from 13 to 22 pairs against a night without clicks.

**What I take from it.** Strengthening slow waves strengthens memory; small sample.

My doubt: 22 people is very few, and the same team sells a headband that plays the clicks. I want to see it repeated by somebody else.

**Details.** Design: each volunteer spent two nights in the unit a week apart, one with clicks and one with the headband silent, in an order set by lot. The clicks did not wake anybody. Deep sleep lasted the same on both nights; its slow waves were larger with the clicks.

**For the seminar.** How would this study change what I do the night before an exam, and the afternoon before? I should bring one concrete habit from it, and one reason to distrust it. Method to remember: who was tested, how long after learning, and against what.

## 5. Haddad school study, later start

**What they did and found.** A secondary school moved its first lesson from 7:30 to 8:45 for one year. 640 pupils slept 41 minutes more on school nights, and their grades in mathematics rose by 0.3 points on a scale of 10.

**What I take from it.** More sleep in teenagers comes with better grades; no control school.

My doubt: a whole year passed, with new teachers and a new mathematics book, and there is no second school to compare with.

**Details.** Design: sleep was measured with a wrist band for two weeks in October of each year. Lateness fell from 9 % to 4 % of first lessons. Pupils went to bed only 11 minutes later than before, which is why they gained sleep. Grades in languages did not change.

**For the seminar.** How would this study change what I do the night before an exam, and the afternoon before? I should bring one concrete habit from it, and one reason to distrust it. Method to remember: who was tested, how long after learning, and against what.

## 6. Bauer and Nwosu, six-minute naps

**What they did and found.** 30 students read a list of 30 words and then stayed awake, napped for 6 minutes or napped for 35 minutes. They recalled 6.9, 8.2 and 9.6 words an hour later.

**What I take from it.** Even a very short nap helps a little; a longer one helps more.

My doubt: an hour later is a short wait. Whether six minutes of sleep still shows the next day is the question I would ask in the seminar.

**Details.** Design: ten in each group, assigned by lot, in a sleep laboratory at 14:00. The six-minute nap was ended by the researcher at the first six minutes of sleep. The authors propose that falling asleep itself starts a process that goes on after waking.

**For the seminar.** How would this study change what I do the night before an exam, and the afternoon before? I should bring one concrete habit from it, and one reason to distrust it. Method to remember: who was tested, how long after learning, and against what.

## 7. Castellanos group, caffeine against naps

**What they did and found.** 54 adults had either 200 mg of caffeine, a 60-minute nap or a placebo after lunch. The nap improved recall of a word list by 12 %; caffeine did not improve recall and made motor learning worse.

**What I take from it.** For memory a nap beats coffee.

My doubt: 200 mg is two cups of coffee; people who drink four a day may react differently, and nobody asked what they usually drink.

**Details.** Design: 18 in each group; three tasks, a word list, a finger-tapping sequence and a texture task, learned at 9:00 and tested at 16:00. The nap helped all three. Caffeine helped none and made the tapping 9 % slower than the placebo.

**For the seminar.** How would this study change what I do the night before an exam, and the afternoon before? I should bring one concrete habit from it, and one reason to distrust it. Method to remember: who was tested, how long after learning, and against what.

## 8. Ivanova cohort, older adults

**What they did and found.** A follow-up of 1,150 adults over 65 for 8 years. Those who slept under 6 hours a night had 1.3 times the risk of a marked memory decline, after adjusting for age, schooling and blood pressure.

**What I take from it.** Short sleep goes with later decline; it does not prove the cause.

My doubt: people already declining may sleep worse, so the arrow could point the other way. Eight years of follow-up helps but does not settle it.

**Details.** Design: sleep was reported by the people themselves at the start and every two years; memory was tested with a story to recall after 20 minutes. Those who slept over 9 hours also had a higher risk, 1.2 times. 14 % left the study before its end.

**For the seminar.** How would this study change what I do the night before an exam, and the afternoon before? I should bring one concrete habit from it, and one reason to distrust it. Method to remember: who was tested, how long after learning, and against what.

## Across the eight

Three of the eight used naps (Lindqvist, Bauer and Nwosu, Castellanos). The largest study is Ivanova's cohort, the smallest the Tanaka sleep unit. For the essay: sleep after learning, naps, and lost sleep that adds up.
