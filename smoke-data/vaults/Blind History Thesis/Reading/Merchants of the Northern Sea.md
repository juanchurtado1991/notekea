# Merchants of the Northern Sea

Thomas Halloran, *Merchants of the Northern Sea: Scottish Houses in the Baltic Trade* (2011).
Read September–October 2026. The closest book to my thesis; I need to be clear where I differ.

## Overall
Halloran argues that a small number of Leith and Dundee houses ran most of the Baltic trade through
family partnerships, and that they survived the end of the war boom by moving from timber to flax.
He is very good on the families and weak on the customs evidence, which he mostly takes from printed
returns. My ledgers should let me test his story ship by ship.

## Chapter notes

### Ch. 1 — The northern sea
Scene-setting. The Forth ports, the Sound tolls, the seasons of ice. Useful map on p. 14 of the
routes from Leith to Memel, Danzig, Riga and Pernau. Nothing I can cite as evidence.

### Ch. 2 — Families and partnerships
How partnerships were formed by marriage and by apprenticeship. The Ord family is one of three main
examples. He uses the NLS Ord letters (MS. 9204) but only the first sixty folios. I have seen the
rest, so there is room to add to him here.

### Ch. 3 — Credit and the bill of exchange
Long and technical. Bills drawn on London houses, the three-month usance, protests for non-payment.
The table on p. 77 of protested bills by year is worth copying into my notes for Chapter 2.

### Ch. 4 — Timber after the war
The colonial preference and the collapse of Baltic timber after 1815, then the partial recovery in
the 1830s. He has the 1830s recovery as small; my Leith figures say it was large for a few houses.

### Ch. 5 — Flax and the east-coast mills
Overlaps with [[The Flax Frontier]], which he cites a lot. The Kirkcaldy spinners appear briefly.

### Ch. 6 — Agents and commissions
The best chapter. Explains the difference between a merchant who owns the cargo and an agent who
takes a commission, and how the same house could be both in one year. This answers one of my
open questions about Ramsay & Ord and the Kirkcaldy cargoes.

### Ch. 7 — Insurance and loss
Lloyd's and the Edinburgh underwriters, wrecks on the Danish coast, a long case study of a cargo lost
off Skagen. Not much for me.

### Ch. 8 — Politics and petitions
The Chamber of Commerce petitions on the timber duties. He quotes the petition I found in GD1/402/5
but dates it a year too late. Worth a polite footnote.

### Ch. 9 — Crisis and failure
The commercial crisis of the 1840s. Several houses fail as timber prices fall after the duty cut and
credit tightens. Case studies on pp. 280–295. The key one for me: Ramsay & Ord, the biggest Leith
timber house of the 1830s, went bankrupt in 1847, with debts of over £60,000, most of them bills on
London. He links the failure directly to the timber duty reduction, which I am not sure about: my
ledgers show them already moving into flax by 1841. The Court of Session inventory (CS96/2/77) should
show what they owned at the end.

### Ch. 10 — Afterlives
What happened to the families. Some moved to Glasgow, some to shipping. Short and anecdotal.

## Where I disagree
- He underrates the 1830s timber boom at Leith.
- He takes printed returns at face value; [[Ports and Paperwork]] shows why that is risky.
- He reads every failure as a timber story; I think flax credit mattered as much.

## Quotes to use (paraphrase first, check page numbers)
- p. 41 on marriage as the "real" partnership contract.
- p. 163 on agents being "merchants in all but risk".
- p. 287 on the end of Ramsay & Ord.
